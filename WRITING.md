# Writing for Enchiridion

This document governs Enchiridion's public copy, supplements, modules, and other original prose. Its purpose is not to make every piece sound the same, but to preserve a common posture toward the reader and the books. This charter is alive. It carries the program’s pedagogy and should evolve through the work of writing and building the curriculum.

## The governing aim

Enchiridion brings readers into direct contact with primary works and equips them to remain there.

The program's writing should remove accidental barriers: an unfamiliar notation, an unavailable instrument, a missing convention, a form of argument the reader has not met. It should not remove the productive difficulty of the work itself. The supplement succeeds when the reader can return to the text with better questions and greater independence, not when the supplement has supplied a finished interpretation.

The public writing serves a related purpose. It should state clearly what the program offers and why it exists without turning education into a list of outcomes or Enchiridion into a product pitch.

## The reader

Write for an intelligent and willing reader who may not have formal preparation in the subject.

Do not confuse unfamiliarity with incapacity. Name real prerequisites, but do not manufacture them. Explain the convention that makes a page legible, then trust the reader to work. A difficult proposition may require an afternoon; a translation may require a lexicon at every word. Neither is a failure.

The reader is self-directed. Instructions therefore need to be usable, sequences need to be honest, and promises need to be bounded. Do not promise fluency, mastery, or transformation where a chapter can offer only orientation and practice.

## Governing principles

### Let the encounter come first

Where possible, let the reader meet the proposition, passage, diagram, experiment, or problem before explaining it.

Begin with the thing itself. Ask the reader to draw the figure, attempt the problem, inspect the wording, make the observation, or translate the line. An explanation given after a real difficulty has appeared can answer a question. The same explanation given beforehand often becomes a substitute for the encounter.

This does not require withholding every definition or instruction. It requires asking whether the reader needs a piece of information in order to begin, or whether we are giving it because we already know it will matter later.

### Scaffold action, not conclusions

Good scaffolding gives the reader something to do. It may supply materials, identify a starting place, divide a long task, recall a prerequisite, or point to a useful passage. It does not carry out the central act of thought in advance.

Prefer prompts such as:

- Draw the construction as it is made.
- Compare these two uses of the word.
- Work the problem before reading the solution.
- Measure what changes and what remains fixed.
- What did the author name, and what did that choice make possible?

A leading question whose answer has already been settled by the prose is an explanation wearing the shape of a question. Use questions to direct attention or hold open a real problem.

### Explain at the point of need

Introduce terminology, notation, and technique when they answer a difficulty the reader has encountered or is about to encounter.

Avoid preliminary inventories whose relevance is not yet visible. A compact reference table may be useful, but it should support reading rather than stand in for it. When a guide grows into a survey of everything worth knowing about its subject, return to the text and ask which parts the reader actually needs.

### Stay with the text

The default posture is textual, not contextual. Enchiridion facilitates an encounter with Archimedes, not with Archimedes as a historical subject.

Do not add biography, intellectual history, scholarly interpretation, later reception, or modern evaluation merely because it is relevant or interesting. Do not explain what a work represents before the reader has had the chance to find out what it does. The curriculum creates context chiefly through sequence and juxtaposition: one book is placed beside another, and the reader is allowed to notice the relation.

Context is warranted when it is necessary to use the text, when the source or transmission bears directly on what is in front of the reader, or when the distinction between evidence and inference is itself the problem under examination. Even then, give no more than the encounter requires and return promptly to the work.

When an inference must be made, distinguish it from what the text states. Write confidently where the evidence is firm, conditionally where it is not, and leave what is unknown unknown. The eventual payoff comes in retrospect, as the student progresses through the program and forms their own context. Do discuss previous threads explicitly covered in the program if they are relevant for comparative or pedagogical purposes.

### Keep old and modern concepts distinct

Modern notation and vocabulary can make an old procedure easier to follow. They can also conceal what is strange or consequential about it.

Do not silently replace a ratio with a fraction, a square with an exponent, comparative thrust with pressure, or a geometric construction with an algebraic equation. First let the historical expression do its work. If a modern translation is useful, name it as a translation and ask what it clarifies, changes, or leaves out.

The goal is not to imitate ancient vocabulary after it has ceased to help. It is to allow the reader to experience the assumptions of different mathematical frameworks without applying the assumptions that come with modern notation.

### Leave live questions open

Do not tidy away a contradiction, missing step, unstable definition, or unexplained choice that the text itself does not resolve. Such difficulties are often the substance of the reading.

A supplement may sharpen the problem, show where it arises, or give the reader the equipment to pursue it. It need not issue a verdict. Endings are often strongest when they return the reader to the work carrying a question rather than close with a summary of lessons learned.

The writer will sometimes know where a question leads. That knowledge may shape a juxtaposition, an omission, or a rhetorical question without being stated outright. A knowledgeable reader may recognize the foreshadowing while a first-time reader encounters only a live question. Used with restraint, this is a kind of Socratic dialogue in prose: outside knowledge deepens the invitation without becoming the subject. It must not make recognition a prerequisite or turn coyness into a performance.

### Treat difficulty honestly

Do not apologize for demanding material, and do not dramatize it as forbidding. State what the task requires, offer a foothold, and let progress be real.

Avoid reassurance that denies the reader's experience. "This is simple" is no help when it is not simple to the person reading. Prefer a precise account: the proof is long but repeats one construction; the vocabulary is small but the syntax is dense; the experiment is conceptually direct but sensitive to measurement error.

### Preserve differences in what the reader is doing

A proof, an observation, a measurement, a reconstruction, and a historical report establish different things.

Honor those differences in the design of the work, but do not ordinarily announce them as an epistemic lesson. Let the proof prove and the trial disclose what happens in this trial. Do not let a later anecdote quietly become evidence that an author performed an experiment.

Often the distinction is better opened as a question: What has the experiment shown that the demonstration did not? What can the report establish? Why does the author accept one kind of result and not another? The difference may itself be the substance of a text or exercise. Preserve enough uncertainty for the reader to find it.

## Writing by form

### Public copy

Public copy should make the size and purpose of Enchiridion legible. It may describe the whole arc: sustained reading in the history of mathematics, science, philosophy, and literature culminating in practical technical ability. It should present technical capability as joined to judgment, taste, historical consciousness, and ethical discernment rather than as the sole end of education.

Write directly. Avoid startup language, institutional grandeur, inflated promises, and defensive qualification. The work may be ambitious without claiming that every reader will complete it or emerge transformed. State what Enchiridion is building, whom it may serve, and what kind of work it asks.

### Reading guides

"Reading guide" is project shorthand for a limited intervention, not a survey, interpretation, summary, or companion to everything in the book. Not every text needs one. A guide exists where a particular barrier prevents an otherwise prepared reader from beginning or continuing: notation obscures the method, a convention goes unstated, the visible page invites the wrong operation, or the form of the argument is unfamiliar.

Build the guide around that barrier, not around everything its writer knows about the subject. Take its purpose from the text, remove no more difficulty than necessary, and get out of the way. A successful guide makes itself progressively less necessary.

Whenever possible:

1. Put the passage or problem before the reader.
2. Let the natural misunderstanding or obstruction appear.
3. Identify the smallest idea, convention, or question that unlocks further reading.
4. Test it against another passage.
5. Return the reader to the work with a habit they can reuse.

### Laboratories

Anchor a lab to a proposition, observation, or procedure in the reading. Keep demonstration and trial distinct in the structure without necessarily lecturing on the distinction. Give complete, safe, workable instructions, but leave room for the reader to predict, measure, compare, and account for discrepancies. Where what the trial can establish is itself at issue, let the result raise the question.

A lab should culminate in a result the reader has produced, not merely witnessed.

### Exercises

Order exercises by the demand they place on the reader, not only by chronology or subject. State the task clearly. Supply answers where they permit useful self-correction; withhold them where the work lies in judgment or reconstruction.

An exercise introduction may identify a feature worth attending to. It should not interpret the passage before the reader begins.

### Modules

A module makes a bounded promise and builds toward independent practice. Each chapter should make the next possible. Support should be withdrawn deliberately, not dropped without warning.

Modules may teach directly where direct instruction is their purpose. Even there, prefer examples drawn from works in the program, reference tools the reader can continue to use, and exercises that return knowledge to practice.

### Metadata and catalog copy

Be factual, compact, and non-promotional. Say what the item contains, what work it accompanies, and what role it plays. Do not use metadata to make claims the material itself does not fulfill.

### Changelogs

Changelogs may carry more narrative voice. They document discoveries, corrections, editorial decisions, and the methods by which the library is made. Preserve uncertainty where it existed and explain consequential judgments, but keep the work rather than the author at the center.

## Prose style

Enchiridion's prose is plainspoken, restrained, serious, and studied.

- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Use simple English where possible. Be mindful of audiences with diverse native languages and the needs of localization.
- Use technical terms and jargon when they make a necessary distinction; define them where they become useful.
- Vary sentence length. Let short sentences carry real emphasis, not manufactured drama.
- Use longer sentences when the reasoning itself unfolds through qualification or contrast.
- Use the second person chiefly to invite action or direct attention.
- Contractions are acceptable when natural.
- Quote the primary text when its exact language matters.
- Prefer specific claims to superlatives.
- Prefer `draw`, `try`, `notice`, `compare`, and `work through` to claims that the reader will `understand`, `appreciate`, or `master` something.
- Use rhetorical questions sparingly. One governing question is stronger than a succession of prompts that tell the reader how to react.
- Avoid motivational filler, academic throat-clearing, and the language of optimization or content delivery.
- Avoid `obviously`, `simply`, and `of course` where a reader may reasonably struggle.
- Do not repeatedly announce that something is famous, elegant, profound, satisfying, or important. Arrange the encounter so that its importance can become perceptible.
- Do not heighten every transition. Restraint gives emphasis somewhere to go.

Clarity does not require a neutral or impersonal voice. The prose may show judgment, pleasure, and conviction. It should not perform them for effect.

## Encounter rehearsal

Before writing or substantially revising a supplement, rehearse the encounter when practical.

1. Use the exact edition in the Enchiridion library.
2. Begin with only the knowledge available at that point in the syllabus.
3. Attempt the passage, proposition, or problem without supplementary explanation.
4. Record the natural guesses, imported assumptions, and points where progress stops.
5. Ask questions until the obstruction is precise.
6. Identify the smallest intervention that would allow the reader to continue.
7. Draft around that intervention.
8. Remove explanations the encounter never required.
9. Test the draft against a fresh attempt.

This rehearsal may be conducted with another person or through a constrained student roleplay with a language model that can read the project text. Its purpose is diagnostic. The conversation discovers where writing is needed; it is not itself the prose to publish.

## Revision questions

Before considering a piece complete, ask:

- Has the reader met the text before being told what to think about it?
- What action does each section enable?
- Is this explanation necessary here?
- Are we supplying context because the encounter requires it, or because we know it?
- Have we translated an old concept into a modern one without marking the change?
- Is a confident statement supported by the text, or is it our inference?
- Does a question direct attention to a real problem, or merely solicit the answer already given?
- Have we closed a question the text leaves open?
- Have we collapsed proof, experiment, observation, reconstruction, or report into one another?
- Have we announced a distinction the reader could more profitably discover?
- Does the ending return the reader to the work?
- Could a paragraph be removed without reducing the reader's ability to proceed?
- Are quotations, links, terminology, instructions, and factual claims verifiable?
- Does the metadata describe what the finished piece actually is?

## Working examples

These are examples of particular strengths, not templates to imitate in every respect.

- `supplements/2-rome-late-antiquity/reading-diophantus/content.md` begins with a problem, discovers the reader's likely modern assumption, and builds the guide around a reusable question. Its encounter design is the example; its more rhetorical passages are not the default prose register.
- `supplements/1-ancient-greece/archimedes-levers-lab/content.md` keeps deductive demonstration and experimental testing distinct and ends by opening a question.
- `mcp/src/instructions.ts` states the seminar posture: conversation partner rather than lecturer, grounded in passages the reader can check.
- The changelog entries show how technical and editorial work can be narrated clearly without losing the decisions and discoveries that gave the work its shape.

Revisit this section after the current editorial work is complete. As stronger materials are written, replace or refine these examples. The guide should remain answerable to the work itself.
