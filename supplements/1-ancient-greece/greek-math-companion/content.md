# Reading Euclid

> An introduction to working through the *Elements*: what Euclid defines, what he asks to be done, and how a construction becomes a demonstration.

Keep the [definitions](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/definitions), [postulates](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/postulates), and [common notions](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/common) open as you begin Book I. Euclid will return to them by number. This guide begins there too, but it will not explain the lists in advance. Read them first.

## Stop at the first hesitation

Take the definitions in order. Stop at the first one that makes you hesitate—not necessarily because you disagree, but because you cannot tell exactly what Euclid is asking you to recognize.

The opening definitions may be enough:

> 1. A point is that of which there is no part.
>
> 2. And a line is a length without breadth.
>
> 3. And the extremities of a line are points.
>
> 5. And a surface is that which has length and breadth only.
>
> 6. And the extremities of a surface are lines.

If a point has no part, what does it mean for points to be the extremities of a line? Does Euclid say that they are parts from which the line is composed, or that they mark where its length ends? Does the repetition from point and line to line and surface settle the question?

Do not hurry to replace these objects with modern definitions. You do not need to decide what a point ultimately is before continuing. It is enough to notice what Euclid has said and what you may have added to it.

Now read the first three postulates:

> 1. Let it have been postulated to draw a straight-line from any point to any point.
>
> 2. And to produce a finite straight-line continuously in a straight-line.
>
> 3. And to draw a circle with any center and radius.

The definitions repeatedly say what something *is*. These postulates say *draw* and *produce*. What has changed? Does producing a straight line create a new object, or continue something already given? Is the postulate telling you that the continuation exists, permitting you to perform it, or doing both?

Hold those questions while you begin the propositions. Their first use will tell you more than another preliminary explanation would.

## Construct the first proposition

Read only the enunciation of [Proposition I.1](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-1):

> To construct an equilateral triangle on a given finite straight-line.

Draw a finite straight line and call it AB. With the definitions, postulates, and common notions before you, try to satisfy the demand. Begin on a blank page rather than by copying the completed diagram.

When you have made an attempt, follow Euclid's construction one act at a time:

1. Draw a circle with center A and radius AB.
2. Draw a circle with center B and radius BA.
3. Call one of their intersections C.
4. Join C to A and C to B.

Add each thing to your figure only when the text adds it. Which postulate permits each act?

At this point the triangle is visible, but Euclid does not stop:

> And since the point A is the center of the circle CDB, AC is equal to AB. Again, since the point B is the center of the circle CAE, BC is equal to BA.

Mark the place where constructing the figure gives way to showing something about it. Why does drawing the triangle not by itself satisfy the demand?

Definition 20 says that an equilateral triangle is one having three equal sides. Definition 15 gives Euclid the two equalities drawn from the circles. Common Notion 1—“Things equal to the same thing are also equal to one another”—gives him the third. What did each of these contribute that the others did not?

The proposition closes:

> Thus, the triangle ABC is equilateral, and has been constructed on the given finite straight-line AB. (Which is) the very thing it was required to do.

Definition 20 has told you what an equilateral triangle is. Proposition I.1 has shown how to construct one on any given finite straight line. Whether the definition also makes a claim about the figure's existence is not a question you need to settle here. The difference in what the definition and the proposition do will become visible in the next proposition.

## How a proposition moves

Propositions in the *Elements* often pass through a recognizable series of parts. The boundaries are not always equally sharp, and not every proposition displays every part in the same way.

1. **Enunciation** (*protasis*) — the general task or claim, before a particular lettered figure is introduced.
2. **Setting-out** (*ekthesis*) — the given objects are named and lettered: “Let AB be the given finite straight-line.”
3. **Specification** (*diorismos*) — the demand is restated for this figure: “It is required to construct an equilateral triangle on AB.”
4. **Construction** (*kataskeuē*) — the lines, circles, points, or other things needed are added.
5. **Demonstration** (*apodeixis*) — Euclid establishes that what was constructed or asserted satisfies the demand.
6. **Conclusion** (*sumperasma*) — the result is stated again as accomplished.

Use these names when they help you locate yourself in a long proposition. Do not force every sentence into a compartment. The more important distinction is between making what the argument needs and showing what follows from it.

Euclid sometimes marks that difference in his closing words. Proposition I.1 ends with “the very thing it was required to **do**” (*poiesai*). [Proposition I.4](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-4) ends with “the very thing it was required to **show**” (*deixai*). Before reading a proposition, ask which kind of demand its enunciation makes.

## Watch the toolkit grow

Now begin [Proposition I.2](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-2):

> To place a straight-line equal to a given straight-line at a given point (as an extremity).

Euclid gives a point A and a straight line BC. He joins A to B, then says:

> Let the equilateral triangle DAB have been constructed upon it.

He cites Proposition I.1 and continues. The circles and equalities of I.1 are not repeated. What has that proposition added to what Euclid may now ask you to do?

Follow the rest of I.2 with its references open. Postulates 1, 2, and 3 permit new acts in the figure. Definition 15 identifies equal radii. Common Notion 3 licenses the removal of equals from equals, and Common Notion 1 completes the final comparison. Try to state the contribution of each citation before moving to the next sentence.

This is how the book grows. A proposition does not merely leave behind a result to remember. It becomes an available move. When a later proposition cites an earlier one, return to it if you cannot reproduce what the citation permits.

## Read only what was granted

Return briefly to Postulate 5:

> If a straight-line falling across two straight-lines makes internal angles on the same side whose sum is less than two right-angles, then the two straight-lines, being produced to infinity, meet on that side.

What does the postulate permit you to conclude when the sum is less than two right angles? What does it say when the sum is equal to two right angles? What does it say when the sum is greater?

Definition 23 calls straight lines parallel when, being in the same plane and produced indefinitely in both directions, they meet in neither direction. Do not turn that definition and Postulate 5 into converses before Euclid does so.

When you reach [Proposition I.28](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-28) and [Proposition I.29](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-29), compare their directions. I.28 begins with facts about the angles and concludes that the lines are parallel. I.29 begins with lines already assumed parallel and establishes facts about the angles made by a line falling across them. Which implication belongs to which proposition? What had to be established before Euclid could move in either direction?

The habit extends beyond parallel lines. A condition may be sufficient without being necessary; a proposition may establish one direction without establishing its converse. Keep the wording of the claim visible while you work through its figure.

## Notice the unannounced move

[Proposition I.4](#/text/euclid-elements?s=book-i/proposition-4) begins with two triangles having two corresponding sides and their enclosed angles equal. This time Euclid does not construct the triangles. He means to show that their bases, the triangles themselves, and their remaining angles are equal.

His demonstration begins:

> For if triangle ABC is applied to triangle DEF, the point A being placed on the point D, and the straight-line AB on DE, then the point B will also coincide with E, on account of AB being equal to DE.

Draw the two triangles separately and follow the application. A is placed on D; AB is placed on DE; the equalities force B to fit on E and C on F. Euclid then says that BC must coincide with EF, since otherwise two straight lines would encompass an area.

Common Notion 4 says:

> Things coinciding with one another are equal to one another.

It tells Euclid what follows when the figures coincide. Which opening principle permits him to apply one triangle to another? What assures you that its sides and angles remain unchanged while it is moved? Where has Euclid said that two straight lines with the same extremities cannot enclose an area?

You may not find an express answer. Mark the move without hurrying to repair it. Application adds nothing to the final figure in the way drawing or producing does. Is Euclid constructing something, or testing one given figure against another?

The Greek behind “coinciding” retains the sense of things *fitting upon one another*. You do not need Greek to follow the demonstration, but the phrase may help you keep the act in view: one figure is applied; its points and lines fit upon their counterparts; their fit permits equality to be inferred.

Proposition I.4 is careful, but its care does not make every accepted operation explicit. As you continue, distinguish a missing explanation from a missed citation. One asks you to look back. The other may be a question the text leaves in your hands.

## Continue with Euclid

For the next propositions, use a separate page and work in the same order each time:

1. Read the enunciation without copying the finished diagram.
2. Mark what is given and what is required.
3. Draw and label only what the text introduces.
4. Notice where construction gives way to demonstration.
5. Follow each citation and say what it permits.
6. Compare the conclusion with the original enunciation.
7. Mark any step you supplied or could not justify, then carry the question forward.

Modern notation may occasionally make a sentence easier to track. Use it when it removes a practical obstruction, then return to the geometrical statement and figure. The purpose is not to make Euclid resemble mathematics you already know. It is to become able to continue without the guide.
