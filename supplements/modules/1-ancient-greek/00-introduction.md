# Chapter 0: Introduction & Expectations

This module teaches you the basics of reading Ancient Greek. By the end, you will be able to sit down with a Greek text, a lexicon, and a grammar reference, and parse your way through it, word by word. You won't be able to speak Greek fluently or pass a classics exam, but you will know how to conduct *tool-assisted reading*.

This module runs seven chapters. It covers enough grammar to make the tools usable and enough vocabulary to make the reading possible. What it does not cover — and what you should not expect — is comprehensive mastery of Greek morphology, syntax, or literary style. The goal of this curriculum is to engage with primary texts and learn about the formal aspects of grammar. The tools exist to make that possible at a level of Greek that would have been inaccessible a generation ago.

## What You Will Learn

**Attic Greek (Chapters 1–6):** This is the classical Greek of Athens in the fifth and fourth centuries BC: the language of Euclid, Plato, Aristotle, Heraclitus, and Sappho. These chapters cover:

- The alphabet and pronunciation (Chapter 1)
- How to use Smyth, Perseus, and the LSJ (Chapter 2)
- The case system and the article (Chapter 3)
- Noun declensions and adjective agreement (Chapter 4)
- Verbs: tense, mood, voice, and the participle (Chapter 5)
- A capstone translation exercise from Plato's *Meno* (Chapter 6)

**Koine Greek (Chapter 7):** The widely-spoken Greek of the Hellenistic period and the language of the New Testament. Koine is a simplified form of Attic Greek. Generally speaking, if you can read Plato, then you can read the Gospels.

## The Euclid-First Approach

This module begins with Euclid. There are several reasons for this. Euclid's vocabulary is small and technical: a few dozen words that recur constantly. His sentence structures are formulaic: definitions, postulates, and proofs follow predictable patterns. The subject matter is concrete and visual, you can draw what he is describing. Lastly, Euclid is *the* primary source of the broader mathematical curriculum, so translating it serves double duty.

You will spend the first four chapters working almost exclusively with Euclid, building grammar knowledge on a familiar and constrained text. In Chapter 5, the vocabulary and reading expand to philosophy and poetry. By Chapter 6, you translate Plato independently.

## The Tools

Three reference tools will accompany you throughout:

- **Smyth's Greek Grammar** — the comprehensive reference grammar for Ancient Greek. You will not read it cover to cover. You will look things up in it constantly. Think of it the way a programmer thinks of language documentation: you consult the relevant section when you need it.

- **Perseus Digital Library** — an online tool that lets you click on any Greek word to see its morphological parsing (case, number, tense, mood, etc.) and dictionary entry. This is your primary reading aid. When you encounter a word you cannot parse, Perseus will parse it for you.

- **The LSJ (Liddell-Scott-Jones) Lexicon** — the standard Greek-English dictionary, available through Perseus. When you need a definition, this is where you find it.

Chapter 2 introduces these tools in detail. For now, just know they exist and that the module is designed around them. You are not expected to parse Greek from memory, rather, you are expected to parse it *with your tools*, efficiently and accurately.

## Exercise Files

In addition to the exercises within each chapter, the module includes standalone exercise files for extended practice:

- **[Euclid Exercises](exercises-euclid.md)** — Translation exercises drawn from Euclid's *Elements*: definitions, postulates, common notions, and selected propositions. You will be ready for these after Chapter 4.
- **[Philosophy Exercises](exercises-philosophy.md)** — Translation passages from Heraclitus, Sappho, Aristotle, and Plato, with links to Perseus for each. You will be ready for Heraclitus and Sappho after Chapter 5; the Aristotle and Plato passages are for exploration after Chapter 6.
- **[Bible Exercises](exercises-bible.md)** — Translation exercises from the Greek New Testament, designed to accompany Chapter 7's Koine transition.

The **[Vocabulary Sheet](vocabulary.md)** file is a cumulative word list organized by domain. Think of it as a reference of important Greek words, not a memorization checklist.

## Time and Pace

Work at whatever pace serves you. Some chapters can be completed in a single sitting; others, especially if you are doing all the exercises, will take longer. The translation exercises are open-ended by design: a single passage might take fifteen minutes or an hour depending on how carefully you consider it.

There is no schedule. If you spend a week on Chapter 4 and a day on Chapter 7, that is fine. The goal is your own edification.
