# Chapter 0: Introduction & Expectations

This module teaches you to read Ancient Greek — not fluently, but functionally. By the end, you will be able to sit down with a Greek text, a lexicon, and a grammar reference, and work your way through it. That is a different skill from speaking Greek, or writing Greek, or passing a classics exam. It is the skill of *tool-assisted reading*: using Perseus, the LSJ lexicon, and Smyth's grammar to parse what is on the page, sentence by sentence, word by word.

This is a crash course, not a replacement for a university classics program. A full ancient Greek sequence typically runs two to three years. This module runs seven chapters. It covers enough grammar to make the tools usable and enough vocabulary to make the reading possible. What it does not cover — and what you should not expect — is comprehensive mastery of Greek morphology, syntax, or literary style. You will finish this module as a beginner who can work independently, not as a classicist.

That is enough. The goal of this curriculum is to read primary texts, and the tools exist to make that possible at a level of Greek that would have been inaccessible a generation ago.

## What You Will Learn

The module has two sections.

**Section 1 (Chapters 1–6): Attic Greek.** This is the classical Greek of Athens in the fifth and fourth centuries BC — the language of Euclid, Plato, Aristotle, Heraclitus, and Sappho (with dialectal differences). You will learn:

- The alphabet and pronunciation (Chapter 1)
- How to use Smyth, Perseus, and the LSJ (Chapter 2)
- The case system and the article (Chapter 3)
- Noun declensions and adjective agreement (Chapter 4)
- Verbs: tense, mood, voice, and the participle (Chapter 5)
- A capstone translation exercise from Plato's *Meno* (Chapter 6)

**Section 2 (Chapter 7): Koine Greek.** The widely-spoken Greek of the Hellenistic period and the language of the New Testament. Koine is a simplified form of the Attic you learn in Section 1 — if you can read Plato, you can read the Gospels.

## The Approach: Euclid First

Most Greek courses begin with prose sentences invented for pedagogical purposes: "The farmer sees the lion." This module begins with Euclid.

There are several reasons for this. Euclid's vocabulary is small and technical — a few dozen words that recur constantly. His sentence structures are formulaic: definitions, postulates, and proofs follow predictable patterns. The subject matter is concrete and visual — you can draw what he is describing. And unlike invented sentences, Euclid's text is real: it is a primary source in the curriculum, and translating it serves double duty.

You will spend the first four chapters working almost exclusively with Euclid, building grammar knowledge on a familiar and constrained text. In Chapter 5, the vocabulary and reading expand to philosophy and poetry. By Chapter 6, you translate Plato independently.

## The Tools

Three reference tools will accompany you throughout:

- **Smyth's Greek Grammar** — the comprehensive reference grammar for Ancient Greek. You will not read it cover to cover. You will look things up in it constantly. Think of it the way a programmer thinks of language documentation: you consult the relevant section when you need it.

- **Perseus Digital Library** — an online tool that lets you click on any Greek word to see its morphological parsing (case, number, tense, mood, etc.) and dictionary entry. This is your primary reading aid. When you encounter a word you cannot parse, Perseus will parse it for you.

- **The LSJ (Liddell-Scott-Jones) Lexicon** — the standard Greek-English dictionary, available through Perseus. When you need a definition, this is where you find it.

Chapter 2 introduces these tools in detail. For now, just know they exist and that the module is designed around them. You are not expected to parse Greek from memory — you are expected to parse it *with your tools*, efficiently and accurately.

## Time and Pace

Work at whatever pace serves you. Some chapters can be completed in a single sitting; others — especially Chapter 5 (verbs) and Chapter 6 (capstone) — will take longer. The translation exercises are open-ended by design: a single passage from Euclid might take fifteen minutes or an hour depending on how carefully you parse each word.

There is no schedule. If you spend a week on Chapter 4 and a day on Chapter 7, that is fine. The goal is comprehension, not speed.
