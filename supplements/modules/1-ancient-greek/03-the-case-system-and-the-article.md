# Chapter 3: The Case System and the Article

In English, word order tells you who does what to whom: "The dog bites the man" means something very different from "The man bites the dog." Greek works differently. Greek marks the *role* of each noun by changing its ending — and the same word can appear almost anywhere in the sentence because its ending, not its position, tells you what it's doing.

These different endings are called *cases*. Understanding what each case does is more important than memorizing the endings themselves. The endings are patterns you will learn to recognize over time (Chapter 4); the cases are concepts you need to grasp now.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain what each of the five Greek cases does
- Recognize the definite article in all its forms and use it to identify case, gender, and number
- Identify what case a noun is in when reading Euclid, using the article and context
- Understand why γραμμή becomes γραμμῆς and σημεῖον becomes σημεῖα

## Why Cases Exist

Consider the English sentence: "The center of the circle is the point." To express the relationship "of the circle," English uses the preposition "of." Greek can do this too — but it can also simply change the ending of "circle" to signal the same relationship, no preposition needed.

This is what cases are: different endings that mark a noun's grammatical role in its sentence. If you have studied Latin, German, Russian, or any other inflected language, this will be familiar territory. If you haven't, it will feel strange at first but becomes natural with practice.

Not all languages work this way. English relies almost entirely on word order; Mandarin Chinese uses context and particles; many languages get by without cases at all. That Greek (along with Latin, Sanskrit, Russian, Finnish, and many others) developed case systems reflects something about how human languages can choose to encode meaning — in the *shape* of words rather than their *position*. The Greek grammarians themselves were fascinated by this. Thrax's classification of cases was one of the first attempts to describe the phenomenon systematically, and the terminology he invented (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative) is still used worldwide today for languages he never encountered. Studying cases is not merely learning Greek grammar — it is learning to see a fundamental dimension of how language can work.

## The Five Cases

### Nominative — The Subject

The nominative is the "default" form of a noun — the form you find in the dictionary. It marks the subject of the sentence: the person or thing doing the action or being described.

> σημεῖόν **ἐστιν**, οὗ μέρος οὐθέν.
>
> "A **point** is that of which there is no part." (Definition 1)

Here σημεῖον (point) is nominative — it is the thing being defined.

### Genitive — "Of," Possession, Source, Separation

The genitive expresses a relationship between two nouns, most commonly translated "of." It covers possession ("the center **of the circle**"), the whole from which a part is taken, source, and separation.

> **γραμμῆς** δὲ πέρατα σημεῖα.
>
> "The limits **of a line** are points." (Definition 3)

Here γραμμῆς is the genitive of γραμμή (line). The ending changed from -ή to -ῆς to signal "of a line." This is the same word you met in Definition 2 (γραμμὴ δὲ μῆκος ἀπλατές, "a line is breadthless length"), where it was nominative.

The genitive is the most versatile Greek case. Smyth devotes pages 312–336 to its uses. For now, "of" will serve you well in most contexts.

### Dative — "To," "For," "With," "By Means Of"

The dative marks the indirect object ("to/for whom"), the instrument or means ("with/by which"), and location ("in/at which"). It is the Swiss Army knife of Greek cases.

In Euclid, you will most often see the dative of instrument or means:

> κέντρῳ μὲν **τῷ** Α διαστήματι δὲ **τῷ** ΑΒ κύκλος γεγράφθω
>
> "Let a circle have been drawn with center A and with radius AB."

Here τῷ Α and τῷ ΑΒ are dative — "with center A," "with radius AB." The dative signals the instrument or specification.

### Accusative — The Direct Object, Extent

The accusative marks the direct object of a verb (the thing acted upon) and extent in space or time.

> Ἠιτήσθω ἀπὸ παντὸς σημείου ἐπὶ πᾶν σημεῖον **εὐθεῖαν γραμμὴν** ἀγαγεῖν.
>
> "Let it be postulated to draw **a straight line** from any point to any point." (Postulate 1)

Here εὐθεῖαν γραμμήν is accusative — the thing being drawn. Notice both the adjective (εὐθεῖαν) and the noun (γραμμήν) changed their endings to match in the accusative.

### Vocative — Direct Address

The vocative is used when speaking directly to someone. It is rare in mathematical and scientific writing but common in Plato's dialogues ("Ὦ Σώκρατες" — "O Socrates") and in Homer. For Euclid, you can largely ignore it.

## Three Genders

Greek nouns come in three genders: **masculine**, **feminine**, and **neuter**. Gender in Greek is a grammatical category, not a natural one — σημεῖον (point) is neuter, γραμμή (line) is feminine, κύκλος (circle) is masculine. There is no deep reason why; it's convention.

Gender matters because adjectives and articles must *agree* with their noun in gender, number, and case. When you see τὴν εὐθεῖαν γραμμήν ("the straight line," accusative), all three words — article, adjective, noun — are feminine accusative singular. This agreement is actually helpful: when you recognize the article's form, it tells you the case, gender, and number of everything that goes with it.

## Two Numbers (Mostly)

Greek has singular and plural, like English. It also has a *dual* number for pairs (two eyes, two hands), but the dual had largely fallen out of use by the classical period. You will occasionally encounter it in older texts, but for practical purposes you can focus on singular and plural.

## The Definite Article: Your Decoder Ring

The definite article (ὁ, ἡ, τό — "the") is the single most useful piece of Greek grammar to learn early. It appears constantly, and because it agrees with its noun in case, gender, and number, it *tells you* what form the noun is in — even when you don't recognize the noun's own ending.

Here is the full article table. This is worth memorizing:

|  | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|--|-----------|----------|--------|
| **Singular** | | | |
| Nominative | ὁ | ἡ | τό |
| Genitive | τοῦ | τῆς | τοῦ |
| Dative | τῷ | τῇ | τῷ |
| Accusative | τόν | τήν | τό |
| **Plural** | | | |
| Nominative | οἱ | αἱ | τά |
| Genitive | τῶν | τῶν | τῶν |
| Dative | τοῖς | ταῖς | τοῖς |
| Accusative | τούς | τάς | τά |

A few things to notice:

**The genitive plural is always τῶν.** Regardless of gender. When you see τῶν, you know: genitive plural.

**The dative always has an iota.** τῷ, τῇ, τῷ, τοῖς, ταῖς, τοῖς. Look for the ι (or iota subscript ῃ, ῷ) — it's a reliable dative signal.

**Neuter nominative and accusative are identical.** τό (singular) and τά (plural). This is true across all of Greek, not just the article: neuter nouns always have the same form in nominative and accusative.

**The article often appears without a noun.** Greek uses the article + genitive where English would use a possessive phrase: τὰ τῆς γραμμῆς πέρατα literally "the of-the-line limits" = "the limits of the line." Learning to read article phrases is a key skill.

## Reading Cases in Euclid

Let's revisit the definitions from Chapter 2's exercises, now with case awareness.

**Definition 1:** Σημεῖόν ἐστιν, οὗ μέρος οὐθέν.

- σημεῖον — neuter nominative singular (the subject: "a point")
- ἐστιν — "is"
- οὗ — genitive relative pronoun ("of which")
- μέρος — neuter nominative singular ("part")
- οὐθέν — "no, nothing"

"A point is that of which no part [exists]."

**Definition 3:** Γραμμῆς δὲ πέρατα σημεῖα.

- γραμμῆς — feminine genitive singular ("of a line")
- δέ — "and/but"
- πέρατα — neuter nominative plural ("limits, boundaries")
- σημεῖα — neuter nominative plural ("points")

"The limits of a line are points."

Notice: γραμμή (nominative, Def. 2) → γραμμῆς (genitive, Def. 3). σημεῖον (nominative singular, Def. 1) → σημεῖα (nominative plural, Def. 3). The endings changed to mark different grammatical roles.

**Proposition I.1 (setting-out):** Ἔστω ἡ δοθεῖσα εὐθεῖα πεπερασμένη ἡ ΑΒ.

- Ἔστω — "let there be" (imperative)
- ἡ ... εὐθεῖα πεπερασμένη — feminine nominative singular: "the given finite straight line"
- ἡ ΑΒ — feminine nominative: "the [line] AB"

Here the article ἡ tells you immediately: this is feminine nominative singular. The noun, adjective, and participle all match.

## How Case, Gender, and Number Work Together

The core principle is **agreement**: the article, any adjectives, and the noun all share the same case, gender, and number. When you see:

> τῇ ΑΒ εὐθείᾳ

You can read it as: τῇ (feminine dative singular) → ΑΒ → εὐθείᾳ (feminine dative singular). The article tells you the whole phrase is feminine dative singular. Even if you didn't recognize εὐθείᾳ as the dative of εὐθεῖα, the article gives it away.

This is why the article is your decoder ring. Learn its forms, and you can read the case of any phrase it appears in.

## Vocabulary

**Case-related terms** (from Thrax and grammatical tradition):

| Greek | Transliteration | Meaning |
|-------|----------------|---------|
| πτῶσις | ptōsis | case (literally "falling" — as in falling away from the nominative) |
| ὀνομαστική | onomastikē | nominative (the "naming" case) |
| γενική | genikē | genitive (the "generic/of" case) |
| δοτική | dotikē | dative (the "giving" case) |
| αἰτιατική | aitiatikē | accusative (the "causal/effected" case) |
| κλητική | klētikē | vocative (the "calling" case) |

These are the terms Thrax uses. You don't need to learn them, but recognizing them connects the English grammatical vocabulary you're building to its Greek origins — every one of these English terms is a translation of the Greek.

**Philosophical vocabulary** (words you will encounter as you begin reading beyond Euclid):

| Greek | Transliteration | Meaning |
|-------|----------------|---------|
| εἶναι | einai | to be (the infinitive of ἐστί) |
| γίγνεσθαι | gignesthai | to become, to come into being |
| δίκη | dikē | justice, custom, right |
| νόμος | nomos | law, custom, convention |
| ἀνάγκη | anankē | necessity |
| αἰτία | aitia | cause, reason, accusation |
| ὕλη | hulē | matter, material (Aristotle's term) |
| εἶδος | eidos | form, kind, idea (Plato and Aristotle) |
| δύναμις | dunamis | power, capacity, potentiality |
| ἐνέργεια | energeia | activity, actuality (Aristotle's term) |

Return to the seven definitions from Chapter 2, Exercise 3. For each definition, identify every noun and its case. Use the article (when present) as your guide, and use Perseus to check your work.

For example, in Definition 4:

> Εὐθεῖα γραμμή ἐστιν, ἥτις ἐξ ἴσου τοῖς ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτῆς σημείοις κεῖται.

- εὐθεῖα γραμμή — nominative (subject)
- τοῖς ... σημείοις — dative plural (τοῖς signals dative, σημείοις is the dative plural of σημεῖον)
- ἑαυτῆς — genitive ("of itself")

### Exercise 2: The Article in Proposition I.1

Open the Elements to Proposition I.1. Read through the proof and circle (or note) every instance of the definite article. For each one:

1. Identify its form from the table above (e.g., τῆς = feminine genitive singular)
2. Find the noun it belongs to
3. State the case and what role it plays (subject, "of"-relationship, instrument, direct object)

You should find the article appearing dozens of times in a single proposition. This is normal — Greek uses the article far more than English does.

### Exercise 5: Genitive Chains

Greek often chains genitives together. In Euclid you will encounter phrases like:

> τὸ κέντρον τοῦ κύκλου

"the center of the circle"

And in philosophical texts:

> ἡ τῆς ψυχῆς ἀρετή

"the virtue of the soul"

Parse the following phrases (identify each word's case and translate):

1. τὰ πέρατα τῆς γραμμῆς
2. αἱ γωνίαι τοῦ τριγώνου
3. ἡ τοῦ κύκλου περιφέρεια
4. τὸ κέντρον τοῦ κύκλου

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. τὰ πέρατα (neuter nominative/accusative plural, "the limits") τῆς γραμμῆς (feminine genitive singular, "of the line") — "the limits of the line"
2. αἱ γωνίαι (feminine nominative plural, "the angles") τοῦ τριγώνου (neuter genitive singular, "of the triangle") — "the angles of the triangle"
3. ἡ (feminine nominative singular) τοῦ κύκλου (masculine genitive singular, "of the circle") περιφέρεια (feminine nominative singular, "circumference") — "the circumference of the circle"
4. τὸ κέντρον (neuter nominative/accusative singular, "the center") τοῦ κύκλου (masculine genitive singular, "of the circle") — "the center of the circle"

Notice in #3 how the genitive phrase τοῦ κύκλου is sandwiched between the article ἡ and its noun περιφέρεια. This is a common Greek word-order pattern — the article signals that a noun is coming, and material can be inserted between them.
