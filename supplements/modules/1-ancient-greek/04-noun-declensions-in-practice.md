# Chapter 4: Noun Declensions in Practice

You now understand what the five cases do and can read the article to identify case, gender, and number. In this chapter you will learn the patterns by which Greek nouns change their endings — the *declensions*. There are three declension groups in Greek. The first two are regular and cover the vast majority of nouns you will encounter in Euclid and early philosophical prose. The third is more varied and can be consulted as needed.

The good news: if you have memorized the article table, you already know most of the first and second declension endings. They echo each other closely.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

- Recognize first and second declension nouns in any case
- Identify a noun's declension from its dictionary entry
- Parse noun phrases in Euclid without relying solely on the article
- Know when to consult Smyth for third declension forms

## How Declensions Work

A *declension* is a classification group — a set of nouns (and adjectives, and pronouns) that change their endings in the same way. Think of it as a conjugation pattern for nouns: once you know which declension a noun belongs to, you know how it will behave in every case and number. The word comes from the Latin *declinatio*, itself a translation of the Greek κλίσις (*klisis*, "bending, inflection").

A Greek noun has two parts: a **stem** (which carries the meaning) and an **ending** (which marks case and number). The stem stays the same; the ending changes.

The three declensions are classified by the vowel (or consonant) at the end of the stem:

- **First declension**: stems ending in -α or -η (mostly feminine, some masculine)
- **Second declension**: stems ending in -ο (mostly masculine and neuter)
- **Third declension**: stems ending in a consonant or -ι/-υ (all genders)

When you look up a noun using Perseus, the morphological tool first shows you the word's parsing (e.g., "noun sg masc nom"). Clicking through to the LSJ entry, you'll see the headword followed by the article indicating gender:

> κύκλος, ὁ

This tells you: κύκλος is masculine (ὁ). The gender and nominative ending together identify the declension — masculine in -ος is second declension.

Traditional print dictionaries include the genitive ending explicitly (κύκλος, -ου, ὁ), and you may see this format in other references. Either way, the key information is the same: nominative form + gender → declension.

Here are the patterns:

> γραμμή, ἡ — feminine (ἡ), nominative in -η → first declension

> κύκλος, ὁ — masculine (ὁ), nominative in -ος → second declension

> σημεῖον, τό — neuter (τό), nominative in -ον → second declension neuter

## First Declension

First declension nouns are mostly feminine. The key distinction is whether the nominative ends in -η or -α.

**Feminine in -η** (like γραμμή, "line"):

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|------|----------|--------|
| Nominative | γραμμ-**ή** | γραμμ-**αί** |
| Genitive | γραμμ-**ῆς** | γραμμ-**ῶν** |
| Dative | γραμμ-**ῇ** | γραμμ-**αῖς** |
| Accusative | γραμμ-**ήν** | γραμμ-**άς** |
| Vocative | γραμμ-**ή** | γραμμ-**αί** |

**Feminine in -α** (like γωνία, "angle"):

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|------|----------|--------|
| Nominative | γωνί-**α** | γωνί-**αι** |
| Genitive | γωνί-**ας** | γωνι-**ῶν** |
| Dative | γωνί-**ᾳ** | γωνί-**αις** |
| Accusative | γωνί-**αν** | γωνί-**ας** |
| Vocative | γωνί-**α** | γωνί-**αι** |

Notice: the **plural forms are identical** for both types. The differences are only in the singular, and they are predictable — η-type uses η throughout, α-type uses α throughout.

Compare these endings with the feminine article (ἡ, τῆς, τῇ, τήν / αἱ, τῶν, ταῖς, τάς). The vowel patterns match.

**Masculine first declension** nouns do exist (like πολίτης, "citizen," or ναύτης, "sailor") — they follow the feminine pattern but with -ς in the nominative singular and -ου in the genitive singular. You will encounter these occasionally; Smyth §223–232 covers them.

## Second Declension

Second declension covers masculine nouns in -ος and neuter nouns in -ον.

**Masculine in -ος** (like κύκλος, "circle"):

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|------|----------|--------|
| Nominative | κύκλ-**ος** | κύκλ-**οι** |
| Genitive | κύκλ-**ου** | κύκλ-**ων** |
| Dative | κύκλ-**ῳ** | κύκλ-**οις** |
| Accusative | κύκλ-**ον** | κύκλ-**ους** |
| Vocative | κύκλ-**ε** | κύκλ-**οι** |

**Neuter in -ον** (like σημεῖον, "point"):

| Case | Singular | Plural |
|------|----------|--------|
| Nominative | σημεῖ-**ον** | σημεῖ-**α** |
| Genitive | σημεί-**ου** | σημεί-**ων** |
| Dative | σημεί-**ῳ** | σημεί-**οις** |
| Accusative | σημεῖ-**ον** | σημεῖ-**α** |
| Vocative | σημεῖ-**ον** | σημεῖ-**α** |

The neuter rule from Chapter 3 is visible here: nominative and accusative are always identical (singular -ον/-ον, plural -α/-α).

Compare with the masculine/neuter article (ὁ, τοῦ, τῷ, τόν / οἱ, τῶν, τοῖς, τούς and τό, τοῦ, τῷ, τό / τά, τῶν, τοῖς, τά). The vowel patterns match again — ο throughout, with the same shifts.

## Third Declension: A Note

The third declension is more varied because its stems end in consonants or unusual vowels. Nouns like βάσις (base), δύναμις (power), and πόλις (city) are third declension. You will encounter these, and when you do, the strategy is:

1. Use Perseus to identify the word and its parsing
2. Note the stem pattern
3. If you need the full paradigm, look up the relevant section in Smyth Part II (§240–285)

You do not need to study third declension systematically at this stage. The first and second declensions, combined with the article, will carry you through most of your reading. When a third declension form comes up, treat it as a lookup task.

## Adjectives

Greek adjectives follow the same declension patterns as nouns — and must agree with their noun in case, gender, and number. Most common adjectives use second declension endings for masculine and neuter, and first declension endings for feminine.

For example, ἴσος, ἴση, ἴσον ("equal"):

- ἴσος (masculine nominative, second declension pattern)
- ἴση (feminine nominative, first declension pattern)
- ἴσον (neuter nominative, second declension pattern)

When Euclid writes ἴσαι ἀλλήλαις ("equal to one another," from Proposition I.6), ἴσαι is feminine nominative plural — matching γωνίαι (angles), which is also feminine nominative plural. The adjective agrees.

You do not need to learn separate adjective paradigms. If you know the first and second declension noun patterns, you know the adjective patterns.

## Putting It Together: Reading Without the Article

The article does not always appear. In Euclid's enunciations and in general statements, the article is often absent. When it is, you rely on the noun's own endings — which you now know.

> Ἐὰν **τριγώνου** αἱ δύο **γωνίαι** **ἴσαι** ἀλλήλαις ὦσιν...

"If two **angles** of a **triangle** are **equal** to one another..." (I.6)

- τριγώνου — second declension neuter, genitive singular (-ου ending). "Of a triangle."
- γωνίαι — first declension feminine, nominative plural (-αι ending). "Angles." (Subject.)
- ἴσαι — first declension feminine, nominative plural (-αι ending). Agrees with γωνίαι.

No articles here except αἱ before δύο. But the endings alone tell you the grammatical relationships.

The workflow for parsing any noun you encounter is now clear: identify the stem (the part that stays constant), look at the ending, and match it to the patterns you've learned. The ending tells you the case and number; the declension pattern tells you where to look if you need confirmation. When in doubt, Perseus will parse it for you — but over time, the common endings will become automatic.

### Exercise 2: Parsing Noun Phrases

Parse each noun in the following phrases from Euclid (identify case, number, gender, and declension). Translate the phrase.

1. τὸ ΑΒΓ τρίγωνον
2. τῆς ΑΒ εὐθείας
3. τοῖς ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτῆς σημείοις
4. αἱ ΓΑ, ΑΒ, ΒΓ εὐθεῖαι
5. κέντρῳ τῷ Α

<summary>Answers</summary>

2. τῆς (fem gen sg) ΑΒ εὐθείας (fem gen sg, 1st decl) — "of the straight line AB"
3. τοῖς (neuter dat pl) ... σημείοις (neuter dat pl, 2nd decl) — "the points on it" (ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτῆς = "on itself")

5. κέντρῳ (neuter dat sg, 2nd decl) τῷ (neuter dat sg) Α — "with center A"

### Exercise 3: Euclid's Common Notions

Read the five Common Notions (κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι) from Book I in Greek. These are short, philosophically rich, and heavily use the dative and genitive:

> α´. Τὰ τῷ αὐτῷ ἴσα καὶ ἀλλήλοις ἐστὶν ἴσα.

"Things equal to the same thing are also equal to one another."

Parse τῷ αὐτῷ (dative: "to the same thing") and ἀλλήλοις (dative: "to one another"). Notice how the dative does the work that English handles with "to."

Work through all five Common Notions with this level of attention.

### Exercise 4: Declining a Noun

Choose three nouns from your vocabulary — one first declension (e.g., γραμμή), one second declension masculine (e.g., κύκλος), and one second declension neuter (e.g., σημεῖον). Write out the full declension table for each: all five cases, singular and plural.

Check your work against Smyth or use the paradigm tables in Part II.

### Exercise 5: Reading Proposition I.5

Proposition I.5 is the "bridge of asses" (pons asinorum) — the proof that the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal. It is longer than I.1 or I.6 but uses vocabulary and constructions you now know well.

Read through it in the interlinear, focusing on:
- Identifying the case of every noun phrase (use the article as your guide)
- Tracking how the same nouns shift case as they move from enunciation → setting-out → construction → proof
- Noting every adjective agreement you can spot

This is a reading exercise, not a translation exercise. Your goal is to follow the *grammar* of the proof, not just its logic.
