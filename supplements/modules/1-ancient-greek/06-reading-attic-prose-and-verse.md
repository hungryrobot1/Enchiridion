# Chapter 6: Reading Attic Prose — Capstone

This is the capstone exercise of the Ancient Greek module.

In Chapters 1 through 5 you learned the alphabet and pronunciation, oriented yourself to Smyth, Perseus, and the LSJ, understood the case system and the article, studied noun declensions and adjective agreement, and worked through the verb system including tense, mood, voice, and the participle. You have translated Euclid's definitions, postulates, and propositions. You have parsed Heraclitus and read Sappho aloud.

There is no new grammar here, you have everything you need. It is now time to apply what you've learned to translating a short passage from Plato.

## The Passage: Plato's *Meno*, 82a–85b

The slave boy scene in the *Meno* is one of the most famous passages in ancient philosophy. Socrates, in conversation with Meno about whether virtue can be taught, calls over an uneducated slave boy and leads him through a geometric problem. This demonstration reveals that the boy can arrive at mathematical truth through questioning alone without having been taught. The philosophical claim is that learning is recollection (ἀνάμνησις): the soul already knows, and needs only to be reminded.

The geometry is this: given a square, construct another square with twice the area. The boy's first instinct is to double the side, but that quadruples the area. Socrates guides him to see that the square on the diagonal is the one with twice the area.

The passage is significant not only for its philosophical argument but for its language. This is Attic prose dialogue — conversational, not mathematical. You will encounter question-and-answer patterns, particles that manage the flow of conversation (ἆρα, οὐκοῦν, τί δέ), and everyday vocabulary alongside the geometric terms you already know. The syntax is simpler than Euclid in some ways (shorter sentences, more repetition) and more complex in others (indirect discourse, conditional reasoning).

## Before You Begin

Read the *Meno* in English first. The full text is in your [library](#/text/plato-meno). Read at least from the beginning through 86c to understand the context of the slave boy passage within the larger dialogue about virtue, knowledge, and recollection.

The geometry is closely related to Proposition I.47 of Euclid's *Elements* (the Pythagorean theorem). If you have not yet translated I.47 in the [Euclid exercises](exercises-euclid.md), do so before beginning this passage.

## The Exercise

Translate *Meno* 82a–85b from Greek, using Perseus and the LSJ as your primary tools and Smyth as your grammar reference. Below are two versions of *Meno* on Perseus:

**Perseus (Hopper view)** — every word is clickable for morphological parsing:
[Meno 82a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0177%3Atext%3DMeno%3Asection%3D82a)

**Scaife Viewer** — interlinear Greek and English, section by section:
[Meno 82 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0059.tlg024.perseus-eng2:82?right=perseus-grc2)

Work through the passage at whatever pace serves you.

Do not expect to translate fluently. The goal, as it has been throughout this module, is to work through the Greek with your tools — parsing each verb, identifying each case, following the article, recognizing the participles — and arrive at comprehension. If a sentence takes you ten minutes, that is ten minutes well spent. Plato wrote this passage two and a half thousand years ago. There is no rush.
