# Translation Exercises: The Bible

*Additional translation passages from the Septuagint and New Testament to accompany Chapter 7 of the Ancient Greek module. You have spent the module reading Euclid, Heraclitus, Sappho, and Plato. Now the same language carries different freight — the Greek of the Jewish and Christian scriptures, shaped by Hebrew thought, theological urgency, and centuries of liturgical use.*

*These passages are invitations, not assignments. Pick what draws you. Some are short enough for a single sitting; others will take sustained work. The New Testament links go to Perseus Hopper, where you can click any word for morphological parsing. The Septuagint links go to Scaife, which provides the Greek text with an English interlinear but without word-by-word parsing — you will need to use the LSJ and Smyth more actively.*

## Septuagint (LXX)

*The Septuagint is the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, produced in Alexandria beginning in the third century BC. Its translators often maintained fidelity to Hebraic structures that sit uneasily in Greek idiom — a feature, not a bug. Reading the LXX, you encounter Greek shaped by a different tradition of thought.*

### Narrative

**Genesis 1:1–5 — Creation**
The opening of the Bible. Sparse, majestic, rhythmic. Notice how the syntax differs from anything you read in Attic prose — the paratactic καί-chains echo the Hebrew *waw*-consecutive.
[Genesis 1:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg001.1st1K-grc1:1?right=perseus-eng2)

**Genesis 7 — The Flood**
Extended narrative with repetitive, liturgical phrasing. The vocabulary is concrete — animals, water, days — and the sentence structure is accessible.
[Genesis 7:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg001.1st1K-grc1:7?right=perseus-eng2)

**Genesis 22 — The Binding of Isaac**
One of the most powerful narratives in the Bible. Terse, restrained prose — what is *not* said matters as much as what is. Pay attention to the dialogue: Abraham's responses are heartbreakingly brief.
[Genesis 22:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg001.1st1K-grc1:22?right=perseus-eng2)

**Exodus 3 — The Burning Bush**
God's self-revelation to Moses. Contains the famous ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ὤν — "I am the one who is" — a phrase that reverberates through the entire biblical tradition and into the Gospel of John.
[Exodus 3:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg002.1st1K-grc1:3?right=perseus-eng2)

**Exodus 25:10–22 — The Ark of the Covenant**
Detailed prescriptive language — "you shall make it thus." An interesting contrast with Euclid's construction language: both are instructions for building, but the authority behind them is entirely different.
[Exodus 25:10 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg002.1st1K-grc1:25?right=perseus-eng2)

**Job 1:1–12 — The Wager**
The frame narrative. Spare prose setting up an extraordinary philosophical situation. The dialogue between God and the Adversary (ὁ διάβολος in the LXX) is chillingly matter-of-fact.
[Job 1:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg032.1st1K-grc1:1?right=perseus-eng2)

### Law

**Deuteronomy 5:1–23 — The Ten Commandments (LXX version)**
Compare with Exodus 20. The legal/imperative mood dominates. Short, direct commands — syntactically the simplest Greek in the Bible.
[Deuteronomy 5:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg005.1st1K-grc1:5?right=perseus-eng2)

### Poetry and Prophecy

**Psalm 23 (LXX: Psalm 22) — The Lord is My Shepherd**
Perhaps the most famous psalm. Short, imagistic, deeply comforting. The LXX numbering differs from the Hebrew by one for most of the Psalter.
[Psalm 22:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg027.1st1K-grc1:23?right=perseus-eng2)

**Psalm 52 (LXX: Psalm 51)**
A psalm of lament and judgment. Sharper in tone than Psalm 23 — good for seeing the range of the Psalter.
[Psalm 51:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg027.1st1K-grc1:52?right=perseus-eng2)

**Isaiah 53 — The Suffering Servant**
One of the most theologically consequential passages in the Hebrew Bible. Read this, then read the Passion narratives in Matthew — the echoes are deliberate and pervasive.
[Isaiah 53:1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg048.1st1K-grc1:53/?right=perseus-eng2)

**Jeremiah 17:5-11**
A short passabe about trusting in the Lord over the intellect and the passions, comparable to the wisdom of Proverbs 3:5.
[Jeremiah 17:7-11 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg049.1st1K-grc1:17/?right=perseus-eng2)

**Jeremiah 25:30–38 — The Lord's Judgment**
Prophetic poetry at its most intense. Dense imagery, long sentences, a rhetorical sweep unlike anything in the NT.
[Jeremiah 25:30 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0527.tlg049.1st1K-grc1:25/?right=perseus-eng2)

### Proverbs — One-Liners

*Short, self-contained sayings. Translate them as you would Heraclitus fragments — one at a time, meditatively. The Greek text is provided directly below; the Hebrew originals behind them give each a double resonance.*

**Proverbs 1:7**
Ἀρχὴ σοφίας φόβος θεοῦ,
σύνεσις δὲ ἀγαθὴ πᾶσι τοῖς ποιοῦσιν αὐτήν·
εὐσεβέα δὲ εἰς θεὸν ἀρχὴ αἰσθήσεως,
σοφίαν δὲ καὶ παιδείαν ἀσεβεῖς ἐξουθενήσουσιν.

**Proverbs 3:5**
ἴσθι πεποιθὼς ἐν ὅλῃ τῇ καρδίᾳ ἐπὶ θεῷ,
ἐπὶ δὲ σῇ σοφίᾳ μὴ ἐπαίρου·

**Proverbs 4:23**
πάσῃ φυλακῇ τήρει σὴν καρδίαν·
ἐκ γὰρ τούτων ἔξοδοι ζωῆς.

**Proverbs 9:10**
ἀρχὴ σοφίας φόβος Κυρίου,
καὶ βουλὴ ἁγίων σύνεσις·
τὸ γὰρ γνῶναι νόμον διανοίας ἐστὶν ἀγαθῆς.

**Proverbs 11:2**
οὗ ἐὰν εἰσέλθῃ ὕβρις, ἐκεῖ καὶ ἀτιμία·
στόμα δὲ ταπεινῶν μελετᾷ σοφίαν.

**Proverbs 15:1**
ὀργὴ ἀπόλλυσιν καὶ φρονίμους,
ἀπόκρισις δὲ ὑποπίπτουσα ἀποστρέφει θυμόν,
λόγος δὲ λυπηρὸς ἐγείρει ὀργάς.

**Proverbs 16:18**
πρὸ συντριβῆς ἡγεῖται ὕβρις,
πρὸ δὲ πτώματος κακοφροσύνη.

**Proverbs 18:14**
θυμὸν ἀνδρὸς πρᾳύνει θεράπων φρόνιμος,
ὀλιγόψυχον δὲ ἄνδρα τίς ὑποίσει;

**Proverbs 22:1**
αἱρετώτερον ὄνομα καλὸν ἢ πλοῦτος πολύς·
ὑπὲρ δὲ ἀργύριον καὶ χρυσίον χάρις ἀγαθή.

**Proverbs 27:17**
σίδηρος σίδηρον ὀξύνει,
ἀνὴρ δὲ παροξύνει πρόσωπον ἑταίρου.

## New Testament

*The New Testament was written in Koine Greek — the language you transitioned to in Chapter 7. Morphological parsing is available on Perseus: click any word to see its form.*

### The Gospels

**John 1:1–18 — The Logos Prologue**
The natural starting point. Deliberately echoes Genesis 1:1 ("In the beginning") while deploying vocabulary any reader of Heraclitus or Plato would recognize — λόγος, φῶς, σκοτία, ἀλήθεια. The syntax is simple. The ideas are not.
[John 1:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DJohn%3Achapter%3D1%3Averse%3D1)

**Matthew 4:1–11 — The Temptation of Christ**
Dialogue between Jesus and the devil in the wilderness. Each temptation is answered with a quotation from Deuteronomy — a good exercise in recognizing LXX echoes within the NT.
[Matthew 4:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMatthew%3Achapter%3D4%3Averse%3D1)

**Matthew 5:1–12 — The Beatitudes**
μακάριοι οἱ... — "Blessed are the..." Repetitive structure makes this one of the most accessible NT passages. Each beatitude is a single sentence, and the pattern is immediately clear.
[Matthew 5:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMatthew%3Achapter%3D5%3Averse%3D1)

**Matthew 26:14–29 — The Last Supper**
Narrative and dialogue interleaved. Contains the words of institution (τοῦτό ἐστιν τὸ σῶμά μου) — some of the most liturgically repeated Greek in history.
[Matthew 26:14 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMatthew%3Achapter%3D26%3Averse%3D14)

**Matthew 27:1–10 — Judas and the Blood Money**
A short, grim narrative. The vocabulary is concrete and the sentences are short — accessible Greek carrying heavy dramatic weight.
[Matthew 27:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DMatthew%3Achapter%3D27%3Averse%3D1)

**Luke 15:11–32 — The Prodigal Son**
One of the great parables. Narrative Greek at its most vivid — the younger son's descent, the father's response, the elder brother's resentment. Accessible vocabulary, mostly concrete.
[Luke 15:11 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DLuke%3Achapter%3D15%3Averse%3D11)

### The Epistles

**Romans 1:1–7 — Pauline Opening**
A single sentence in Greek — seven verses long. Paul's periodic style at its most elaborate. Compare with the paratactic simplicity of John. Two very different ways of using Koine.
[Romans 1:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DRomans%3Achapter%3D1%3Averse%3D1)

**Romans 12 — The Living Sacrifice**
Paul at his most practical and exhortative. Shorter sentences than his usual style, imperative mood throughout. A good bridge between the narrative Greek of the Gospels and Paul's more complex prose.
[Romans 12:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DRomans%3Achapter%3D12%3Averse%3D1)

**1 Corinthians 13 — Paul on Love**
The ἀγάπη chapter. Rhetorically polished, building to a climax — Paul writing at the height of his literary power. More syntactically complex than the Gospels, closer to Attic periodic style.
[1 Corinthians 13:1 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DI+Corinthians%3Achapter%3D13%3Averse%3D1)

### Revelation

**Revelation 22:6–21 — The Ending**
The final words of the New Testament. Visionary, urgent, liturgical. ἔρχου, κύριε Ἰησοῦ — "Come, Lord Jesus." A fitting place to end.
[Revelation 22:6 on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0155%3Abook%3DRevelation%3Achapter%3D22%3Averse%3D6)
