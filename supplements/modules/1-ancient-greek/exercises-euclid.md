# Translation Exercises: Euclid

*Additional translation passages from Euclid's Elements to accompany the Ancient Greek module. The Elements is your primary Greek text from Chapter 1 onward — these exercises give you more material to work through at your own pace.*

*The interlinear translation in your library (`texts/1-ancient-greece/euclid-elements`) is your main reference. Work from the Greek, consult the LSJ for unfamiliar words, and check yourself against the English. ## Book I: Definitions

*Definitions 1–23. Short, formulaic statements — ideal for early practice with cases and the article. Each definition is a single sentence or two. You already know most of the geometric vocabulary from the module's vocabulary list.*

The definitions establish Euclid's terms: point, line, surface, angle, circle, triangle, square, parallel lines, and the various kinds of angles and figures. They are the simplest Greek in the Elements — almost no verbs, mostly copulative constructions ("A point is that which has no part").

Work through all 23. They go quickly.

---

## Book I: Postulates and Common Notions

*Postulates 1–5, Common Notions 1–5. Philosophically dense in minimal Greek.*

The five postulates are requests: "let it be demanded that..." (ᾐτήσθω). They ask for permission to draw lines, extend lines, describe circles, and assert that all right angles are equal. The fifth postulate — the parallel postulate — is the most famous axiom in the history of mathematics. Its Greek is longer and more syntactically involved than the other four. It will repay careful parsing.

The common notions are self-evident truths: things equal to the same thing are equal to each other; the whole is greater than the part. They read almost like proverbs.

## Book I: Selected Propositions

### Proposition I.1 — Constructing an Equilateral Triangle

*The very first proposition in the Elements.* Given a finite straight line, construct an equilateral triangle upon it. This is a *construction* (πρόβλημα), not a theorem (θεώρημα), so it ends with ὅπερ ἔδει ποιῆσαι (Q.E.F. — "which was to be done") rather than ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι (Q.E.D. — "which was to be shown"). Short, elegant, and discussed in the module chapters. Here, you will fully translate it.

### Proposition I.4 — SAS Congruence

*If two triangles have two sides equal to two sides respectively, and have the angles contained by the equal sides also equal, then they will also have the base equal to the base, the triangle will be equal to the triangle, and the remaining angles will be equal to the remaining angles.* Rich in participles and geometric vocabulary. The proof uses the method of superposition (ἐφαρμόζω — "to fit upon"), which is philosophically interesting: Euclid picks up one triangle and places it on top of the other.

### Proposition I.5 — Isosceles Base Angles (the "Bridge of Asses")

*In isosceles triangles, the angles at the base are equal to one another.* The longest proof so far in the Elements, and historically the point where many students gave up — hence the medieval nickname *pons asinorum*. Grammatically, this is a step up: longer sentences, nested constructions, and a proof that requires following several auxiliary lines.

### Proposition I.32 — The Angle Sum of a Triangle

*In any triangle, the exterior angle is equal to the two interior and opposite angles, and the three interior angles of the triangle are equal to two right angles.* A deeply satisfying result. The proof uses parallel lines (Proposition I.29), so it depends on the parallel postulate. The Greek is moderate in difficulty.

### Proposition I.41 — Parallelogram Double Triangle

*If a parallelogram and a triangle have the same base and are between the same parallels, the parallelogram is double the triangle.* Brief and elegant — a key lemma used in the proof of I.47. The sentence structure is clean and the proof is short.

### Proposition I.47 — The Pythagorean Theorem

*In right-angled triangles, the square on the side subtending the right angle is equal to the squares on the sides containing the right angle.* The culmination of Book I. The proof is long and intricate, involving multiple auxiliary constructions and appeals to earlier propositions. This is the most challenging translation exercise from Book I, but by this point you will have encountered all of its vocabulary and grammatical patterns in simpler contexts. You will have read this proposition in English as part of the main curriculum — the Greek is a chance to see how Euclid himself said it.

## Book V: Definitions — Ratio and Proportion

*Definitions 1–5 (at minimum). The conceptual foundation for the theory of proportion.*

Book V is where Euclid handles ratios between magnitudes — a theory attributed to Eudoxus. The definitions introduce λόγος (ratio), ἀναλογία (proportion), and the famous Definition 5, which defines when magnitudes are "in the same ratio." Definition 5 is one of the most important single sentences in the history of mathematics: it provides a rigorous treatment of proportion that handles incommensurable magnitudes — a problem the Pythagoreans had discovered but not solved.

The Greek is more abstract than Book I — fewer triangles and circles, more talk of "magnitudes" (μεγέθη) and "multiples" (πολλαπλάσια). But the sentence structures are the same ones you learned in the definitions of Book I.

## Book VII: Definitions — Number Theory

*Definitions 1–23. Euclid's arithmetic vocabulary.*

Book VII marks a shift from geometry to arithmetic. The definitions introduce unit (μονάς), number (ἀριθμός), even (ἄρτιος), odd (περιττός), prime (πρῶτος ἀριθμός), composite, and the various relations between numbers. Many of these Greek terms are the direct ancestors of modern mathematical vocabulary.

These definitions are short and formulaic, like Book I's — accessible Greek with conceptually rich content. They also introduce vocabulary that recurs throughout the number-theoretic books (VII–IX).

---

## Book X: Definitions — Commensurability and Irrationality

*Definitions 1–4. The groundwork for Euclid's treatment of irrational magnitudes.*

Book X is the longest and most technical book of the Elements. Its four opening definitions establish the key distinctions: commensurable (σύμμετρα) and incommensurable (ἀσύμμετρα) magnitudes, and rational (ῥητά) and irrational (ἄλογα) magnitudes. These four short statements set up the entire investigation.

The vocabulary is striking: ἄλογος literally means "without ratio" or "without reason" — the same word used elsewhere in Greek for "irrational" in the philosophical sense. The discovery that some magnitudes have no common measure was one of the great intellectual shocks of Greek mathematics.

---

## Book X: Proposition 10 — The Existence of Incommensurable Magnitudes

*To find two straight lines incommensurable with a given straight line, one commensurable in square only, the other incommensurable in square also.* This proposition gestures at one of the deepest results in ancient mathematics: that irrational magnitudes exist, and that they come in different kinds. The proof is constructive and relatively short. It brings together the vocabulary from the Book X definitions and gives concrete mathematical meaning to the abstract distinctions established there.

*The Elements is a vast work — thirteen books, over 450 propositions. These exercises sample its range: the geometric foundations of Book I, the theory of proportion in Book V, the number theory of Book VII, and the exploration of irrationality in Book X. If you find yourself drawn deeper into any of these, the full Greek text is in your library, waiting.*
