## Heraclitus — Fragments

*Short, dense, oracular. Heraclitus writes in a compressed style that resists easy translation — each fragment is a puzzle. The Greek text is provided directly; translate them one at a time, as you would the Proverbs.*

*Fragments are numbered following the Diels-Kranz system. See Burnet's edition in your references for more, or consult the digital copy (interlinerar provided) from [heraclitusfragments.com](https://heraclitusfragments.com/files/ge.html).*

**Fragment 1** (opening of his book)
τοῦ δὲ λόγου τοῦδ ἐόντος ἀεὶ ἀξύνετοι γίνονται ἄνθρωποι καὶ πρόσθεν ἢ ἀκοῦσαι καὶ ἀκούσαντες τὸ πρῶτον· γινομένων γὰρ πάντων κατὰ τὸν λόγον τόνδε ἀπείροισιν ἐοίκασι πειρώμενοι καὶ ἐπέων καὶ ἔργων τοιούτων ὁκοίων ἐγὼ διηγεῦμαι κατὰ φύσιν διαιρέων ἕκαστον καὶ φράζων ὅκως ἔχει· τοὺς δὲ ἄλλους ἀνθρώπους λανθάνει ὁκόσα ἐγερθέντες ποιοῦσιν ὅκωσπερ ὁκόσα εὕδοντες ἐπιλανθάνονται

**Fragment 12** (ever-changing river)
ποταμοῖσι τοῖσιν αὐτοῖσιν ἐμβαίνουσιν ἕτερα καὶ ἕτερα ὕδατα ἐπιρρεῖ

**Fragment 30** (the ever-living fire)
κόσμον τόνδε, τὸν αὐτὸν ἁπάντων, οὔτε τις θεῶν οὐτε ἀνθρώπων ἐποίησεν, ἀλλ' ἦν ἀεὶ καὶ ἔστιν καὶ ἔσται πῦρ ἀείζωον, ἁπτόμενον μέτρα καὶ ἀποσβεννύμενον μέτρα

**Fragment 45** (the soul's limits)
ψυχῆς πείρατα ἰὼν οὐκ ἂν ἐξεύροιο πᾶσαν ἐπιπορευόμενος ὁδόν· οὕτω βαθὺν λόγον ἔχει

**Fragment 46** (thinking and seeing)
τήν τε οἴησιν ἱερὰν νόσον ἔλεγε καὶ τὴν ὅρασιν ψεύδεσθαι

**Fragment 50** (the unity of all things)
οὐκ ἐμοῦ ἀλλὰ τοῦ λόγου ἀκούσαντας ὁμολογεῖν σοφόν ἐστιν ἓν πάντα εἶναι

**Fragment 53** (war as father)
Πόλεμος πάντων μὲν πατήρ ἐστι, πάντων δὲ βασιλεύς, καὶ τοὺς μὲν θεοὺς ἔδειξε, τοὺς δὲ ἀνθρώπους, τοὺς μὲν δούλους ἐποίησε, τοὺς δὲ ἐλευθέρους

**Fragment 80** (strife is justice)
εἰδέναι δὲ χρὴ τὸν πόλεμον ἐόντα ξυνόν, καὶ δίκην ἔριν, καὶ γινόμενα πάντα κατ' ἔριν καὶ χρεών

**Fragment 93** (hiding folly)
ἀμαθίην κρύπτειν ἄμεινον

**Fragment 112** (thinking well is excellence)
σωφρονεῖν ἀρετὴ μεγίστη καὶ σοφίη ἀληθέα λέγειν καὶ ποιεῖν κατὰ φύσιν ἐπαΐοντας

**Fragment 119** (character is fate)
Ἡράκλειτος ἔφη ὡς ἦθος ἀνθρώπῳ δαίμων

**Fragment 123** (nature loves to hide)
φύσις κρύπτεσθαι φιλεῖ.

## Sappho — Selected Fragments

*Lyric poetry in the Aeolic dialect. Sappho's Greek differs from the Attic you have been learning — different vowel sounds, different verb forms, a different music. But the core grammar is the same, and the emotional directness of her language makes it surprisingly accessible. Consult the Wharton edition in your references for text and commentary.*

*Fragment numbering follows the Lobel-Page (LP) system, which is the standard in modern scholarship. The Wharton edition and some older online sources use different numbering — cross-reference by first line if in doubt.*

Digital copies with Greek and English can be found at [digitalsappho.org](https://digitalsappho.org/)

**Fragment 1** (Hymn to Aphrodite)
The only complete poem of Sappho's that survives. A prayer to Aphrodite, structured as a dialogue between the poet and the goddess.

ποικιλόθρον’ ἀθανάτ’ Ἀφρόδιτα,
παῖ Δίος δολόπλοκε, λίσσομαί σε,
μή μ’ ἄσαισι μηδ’ ὀνίαισι δάμνα,
πότνια, θῦμον,

ἔκλυες, πάτρος δὲ δόμον λίποισα
χρύσιον ἦλθες

ἄρμ’ ὐπασδεύξαισα· κάλοι δέ σ’ ἆγον

A description of the physical symptoms of desire — or possibly of jealousy. Referenced in Chapter 5 of the module.

**Fragment 47** (Eros the shaker)
A two-line simile. Compact, violent, imagistic — the syntax is a single comparison.

Ἔρος δ᾿ ἐτίναξέ μοι
φρένας, ὠς ἄνεμος κὰτ ὄρος δρύσιν ἐμπέτων.

**Fragment 57** (the country girl)
A sharp, mocking fragment — Sappho ridicules a rustic woman who enchants someone's mind but doesn't know how to pull her dress over her ankles.

τίς δ᾿ ἀγροΐωτις θέλγει νόον . . .
ἀγροΐωτιν ἐπεμμένα στόλαν . . .
οὐκ ἐπισταμένα τὰ βράκε ᾿ ἔλκην ἐπὶ τὼν σφύρων;

**Fragment 114** (a wedding dialogue)
A brief exchange between a bride and her virginity — a genre piece from Sappho's epithalamia (wedding songs).

(νύμφη). παρθενία, παρθενία, ποῖ με λίποισ’ ἀποίχῃ;
(παρθενία). οὐκέτι ἤξω πρὸς σέ, οὐκέτι ἤξω

**Fragment 120** (a gentle temperament)
A brief self-characterization. The contrast between παλιγκότων (spiteful) and ἀβάκην (gentle, quiet) is clear even in these damaged lines.

ἀλλά τις οὐκ ἔμμι παλιγκότων
ὄργαν, ἀλλ’ ἀβάκην τὰν φρέν’ ἔχω . . .

**Fragment 130** (bittersweet Eros)
The source of the word γλυκύπικρον — "sweetbitter" — which has entered modern languages through this very fragment. Eros is limb-loosening (λυσιμέλης), irresistible, a creeping thing. Two lines dense with compound adjectives.

Ἔρος δηὖτέ μ’ ὀ λυσιμέλης δόνει,
γλυκύπικρον ἀμάχανον ὄρπετον

**Fragment 168B** (the midnight poem)
Four lines of absolute simplicity and loneliness. The most frequently anthologized of Sappho's shorter fragments.

δέδυκε μὲν ἀ σελάννα
καὶ Πληΐαδες· μέσαι δὲ
νύκτες, παρὰ δ’ ἔρχετ’ ὤρα,
ἔγω δὲ μόνα κατεύδω.

## Aristotle — Selected Passages

*Aristotle's prose is dense, technical, and precise — the opposite of Heraclitus. Where Heraclitus compresses, Aristotle distinguishes. His sentences are often long but logically structured: if you follow the argument, the grammar follows. These passages introduce key Aristotelian concepts that recur throughout the curriculum.*

**Metaphysics 980a21 — "All human beings by nature desire to know"**
πάντες ἄνθρωποι τοῦ εἰδέναι ὀρέγονται φύσει. The opening sentence of the Metaphysics. One line, six words, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. A perfect warm-up exercise.
[Metaphysics 980a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0051%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D980a)

**Physics II.3, 194b16–195a3 — The Four Causes**
Aristotle's account of the four kinds of cause (αἰτία): material, formal, efficient, and final. This is a compact passage — roughly 15 lines — where he lists and explains each one with examples. The four causes are among the most important concepts in the entire curriculum; translating the passage where Aristotle introduces them is worth the effort.
[Physics II.3 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0086.tlg031.1st1K-grc1:2.3/)

**Nicomachean Ethics I.7, 1097b22–1098a20 — The Function Argument**
What is the human good? Aristotle argues that it must be connected to the ἔργον (function, characteristic activity) of a human being — just as the good of a flute-player lies in playing the flute well. The argument is tightly structured and moderate in length. Key vocabulary: ἔργον, ἐνέργεια, ἀρετή.
[NE 1097b on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0053%3Abekker+page%3D1097b)

**Nicomachean Ethics IV.3, 1123a34–1124a4 — Megalopsuchia (Greatness of Soul)**
Aristotle's portrait of the magnanimous man — one of the most discussed (and debated) passages in Greek ethics. Even the opening definition is revealing: the megalopsuchos is someone who "thinks himself worthy of great things and is worthy of them." The full chapter is long, but this opening section is self-contained.
[NE 1123a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0053%3Abekker+page%3D1123a)

**Nicomachean Ethics VII.1–2, 1145a15–1146a9 — Akrasia (Weakness of Will)**
The problem statement: how is it possible to know what is good and yet fail to do it? Aristotle surveys earlier views (especially Socrates' claim that no one does wrong willingly) before giving his own account. Dense with philosophical vocabulary but structured as a clear dialectical survey.
[NE 1145a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0053%3Abekker+page%3D1145a)

**De Anima II.1, 412a3–412b6 — The Definition of the Soul**
"The soul is the first actuality (ἐντελέχεια) of a natural body that has life potentially." One of the most compressed and consequential definitions in the history of philosophy. Short but syntactically demanding — every word matters. This is where ἐντελέχεια enters the philosophical lexicon.
[De Anima II.1 on Scaife](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0086.tlg002.1st1K-grc1:2.1/)

## Plato — Selected Passages

*Plato's dialogues are conversations, and his Greek reads like speech — questions, interruptions, ironic asides. The syntax is more fluid than Aristotle's but can be deceptively complex, especially when Socrates builds an argument through nested conditional clauses. The Meno slave boy passage is the module's capstone; the others are for further exploration.*

**Apology 28d–29a — Socrates on Death**
"No one knows whether death may not be the greatest of all blessings for a man, yet they fear it as if they knew it to be the greatest of evils." Compact, rhetorically powerful, and philosophically foundational — the argument that fearing the unknown is a kind of false wisdom.
[Apology 28d on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0169%3Atext%3DApol.%3Asection%3D28d)

**Apology 38a — The Unexamined Life**
ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ. Perhaps the single most quoted line in Greek philosophy. Translate it, then sit with the claim.
[Apology 38a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0169%3Atext%3DApol.%3Asection%3D38a)

**Symposium 201d–202a — Diotima's Dialectic**
Socrates recounts his conversation with Diotima about the nature of love (ἔρως). The passage is structured as rapid question-and-answer — short sentences, clear logic, building to the conclusion that Love is neither beautiful nor ugly, neither wise nor ignorant, but something in between (μεταξύ). Good practice for parsing dialogue.
[Symposium 201d on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0173%3Atext%3DSym.%3Asection%3D201d)

**Meno 82b–85b — The Slave Boy Passage** *(Module Capstone)*
Socrates leads an uneducated slave boy through the discovery that the square on the diagonal is double the original square — using only questions. This is the primary translation exercise for Chapter 6 of the module. It combines mathematical vocabulary (from Euclid) with philosophical dialectic, and the Greek is accessible because Socrates is deliberately speaking simply.
[Meno 82b on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0177%3Atext%3DMeno%3Asection%3D82b)

**Phaedrus 246a–247c — The Charioteer Myth**
The soul as a charioteer driving two winged horses — one noble, one unruly. Vivid mythological imagery, narrative structure, and some of Plato's most beautiful prose. The vocabulary is concrete (wings, horses, chariots, the heavens) even though the subject is metaphysical.
[Phaedrus 246a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0173%3Atext%3DPhaedrus%3Asection%3D246a)

**Republic 514a–515c — The Allegory of the Cave**
"Imagine human beings living in an underground dwelling..." The most famous passage in the history of philosophy. The opening setup — the prisoners, the shadows, the fire — is narrative and visual, making it more accessible than Plato's more dialectical writing. Translating this passage from the Greek, after having read it in English, is one of those experiences where the original language genuinely deepens understanding.
[Republic 514a on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0167%3Abook%3D7%3Asection%3D514a)

**Timaeus 27d–29d — Being and Becoming**
Timaeus lays out the cosmological framework: what always *is* (τὸ ὂν ἀεί) and never becomes, vs. what always *becomes* (τὸ γιγνόμενον) and never is. The first is grasped by νοῦς through λόγος; the second by δόξα through αἴσθησις. Then the Demiurge enters — the divine craftsman who fashions the cosmos as an εἰκών (image) of the eternal παράδειγμα (model). This passage ties together εἶδος, ἐπιστήμη, δόξα, νοῦς, and αἴσθησις into a single cosmological vision. Syntactically among the harder Plato — long periodic sentences with nested relative clauses. A post-capstone challenge.
[Timaeus 27d on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0179%3Atext%3DTim.%3Asection%3D27d)

## One-Liners and Famous Expressions

*Translate these on sight. Many are paraphrases or condensations rather than direct quotations, but they capture ideas that defined Greek thought. Each one is a meditation.*

- γνῶθι σεαυτόν — "Know thyself" (Delphic maxim)
- μηδὲν ἄγαν — "Nothing in excess" (Delphic maxim)
- ἓν οἶδα ὅτι οὐδὲν οἶδα — "I know that I know nothing" (Socrates, paraphrased)
- πάντα ῥεῖ — "Everything flows" (attributed to Heraclitus)
- ἄνθρωπος μέτρον — "Man is the measure" (Protagoras)
- ἀρχὴ ἥμισυ παντός — "The beginning is half of the whole" (proverb)
- μέτρον ἄριστον — "Moderation is best" (Cleobulus)
- αἰὲν ἀριστεύειν — "Always to excel" (Homer, Iliad VI.208)
